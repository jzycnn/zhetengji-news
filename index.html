Oklahoma’s sweepstakes casino ban starts on November 1, and operators are not waiting for the date. The law’s reach down the supply chain sets it apart. Liability extends beyond operators to suppliers, payment processors, geolocation providers and affiliates. Tokens and similar currencies used in dual-currency models count as “representatives of value.” Tribal gaming approved under the federal Indian Gaming Regulatory Act is exempt. Lawmakers overrode Governor Stitt to pass the ban Senator Todd Gollihare authored SB 1589. It had its first Senate reading on February 2, 2026. On March 25, 2026, Representative Kyle Hilbert replaced Representative Fetgatter as its principal House author. The measure changes the scope of an existing gambling offense in the state’s crimes and punishments code. Stitt’s veto letter objected that the bill needlessly created a new felony. He also opposed extending criminal liability to businesses and service providers. In his veto message, he argued that Oklahoma’s gaming laws should be clear, targeted and fair. SB 1589, he said, did not meet that standard. The legislature disagreed. In the Senate, Gollihare moved that the bill become law despite the veto. The motion passed on a roll-call vote. The House then passed the measure over the veto by two-thirds of its elected members. That threshold is set by Article VI, Section 11 of the state constitution. The provisions the governor objected to are now the ones that matter most for the industry. Businesses that serve sweepstakes brands, from payment firms to geolocation vendors, now face felony exposure in the state. That raises the legal risk across the sector, not just for the brands themselves. Players face a different problem. The rules on remaining balances and pending redemptions are unclear. Oklahoma has no regulated online casino. As a result, legal online play in the state now goes only to tribal gaming.
