Image credit: MacRumors forum member sniffies If you'd rather keep the logo dark, Apple has shared a way to turn it off. Unusually though, there's no switch in System Settings. Instead, you need to enter a command in the Terminal app. If that sounds intimidating, don't worry – it's a single line that you can copy and paste. Note that the following command only works on MacBook Pro models with a PROLED XDR display. Open Terminal on your Mac: it's in the Applications ➝ Utilities folder, or you can search for "Terminal" using Spotlight (Command + Space). Type or paste the following command, then press Enter: sudo defaults write com.apple.CoreBrightness LEBacklightMode -int 0 When prompted, enter your administrator password and press Enter. (Terminal doesn't show the password as you type it.) After performing the steps, the logo illumination should turn off immediately, no restart required. The change applies to all macOS user accounts on the machine, and it will carry over even if you start up from macOS Recovery. If you later migrate to a new Mac, the setting even transfers across with the rest of your system. Relight the Apple Logo Reinstalling macOS turns the illumination back on, but you can also turn it back on yourself at any time by entering the following command in Terminal: sudo defaults delete com.apple.CoreBrightness LEBacklightMode Remember to press Enter! Backstory The first Mac laptop with a lit-up Apple logo was the third-generation PowerBook G3 launched in 1999. The glowing symbol proved a mainstay through consecutive Apple notebooks for the next 16 years. The only change in that period was that the logo got flipped upright, so that it no longer appeared upside-down to onlookers when someone was sat in public with the lid open. According to former Apple employee Joe Moreno, the original orientation was deliberate: Apple's design group had found that users kept trying to open their laptops from the wrong end. "Steve Jobs always focused on providing the best possible user experience and believed that it was more important to satisfy the user than the onlooker," Moreno recalled. Ironically, Jobs later reversed the decision. "Opening a laptop from the wrong end is a self-correcting problem that only lasts a few seconds," said Moreno. "However, viewing the upside logo is a problem that lasts indefinitely." Micro contracts and scorigami The two letters target different products. Novig was told that its micro contracts, such as a market on which player opens the scoring in a basketball game, are open to manipulation. ProphetX was told that its scorigami contracts, which settle on whether a game finishes with a scoreline that has never occurred in that sport, do not meet the Commodity Exchange Act’s definition of a swap. Sources told Front Office Sports that the letters describe the filings as false self-certifications, the term used when an exchange certifies a contract that may not be permitted under the statute. Neither set of contracts had been listed, so no customer has traded on them. ProphetX framed the request more narrowly. A company spokesperson said CFTC staff “requested that we voluntarily defer listing the Scorigami contracts pending further review, not that we withdraw them.” The company said it complied, has since supplied further analysis on the contracts’ compliance and economic utility, and remains in contact with agency staff. Novig did not immediately respond to a request for comment, the outlet reported. A signal to the wider market Under the current framework, exchanges can self-certify and list new contracts without prior approval, and the CFTC can step in afterward.
